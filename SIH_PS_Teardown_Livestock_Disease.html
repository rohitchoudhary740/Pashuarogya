01

Pain Points & Core Understanding

What exact problem is being addressed?A broken feedback loop, not a missing diagnosis tool

Strip the problem statement down and it is not asking for an app that diagnoses cattle diseases. It is asking us to close a feedback loop that currently takes weeks and should take hours. Every stage of that loop leaks time, and the disease uses the leaked time to spread.

Loop stageHow it works today in rural MaharashtraTypical lagWhat the PS wants instead
1. SignalFarmer notices an animal is off feed or feverish, waits, tries a home remedy, asks a neighbour1–4 daysSame-hour capture, initiated by the farmer
2. ReportTold verbally to a para-vet on his next village round; written into a paper register1–7 daysStructured, geo-tagged, works offline
3. DetectDistrict officer notices a pattern only once deaths accumulate and rumours travel1–3 weeksAutomated statistical cluster flag at village level
4. ConfirmSample sent to a distant district or state lab; no tracking, no status back to the farmer3–14 daysTracked chain-of-custody with a referral workflow
5. ActRing vaccination and movement control — issued after the spread has happened2–5 weeksPre-emptive, targeted, and verified as executed
The framing to memorise

The disease travels faster than the paperwork. Each day of reporting delay converts a containable village event into a district emergency. India's 2022 Lumpy Skin Disease wave is the proof: over 97,000 cattle died in three months (July to late September 2022), and Maharashtra alone lost 34,711 head across 2022–23 — the second-worst affected state after Rajasthan.

Why does this problem exist?Six root causes, in order of how much they matter

  1. The person who sees the symptom first has no channel. Bharat Pashudhan — the national livestock database — is a field-worker application. Roughly four lakh field workers use it; farmers do not. The signal originates outside the system that needs it, so the clock only starts when a para-vet next reaches the village.
  2. Reporting is punitive rather than protective. Declaring a suspected outbreak can trigger movement bans, market closure, and culling. Farmers under-report for entirely rational economic reasons. Any design that ignores this incentive will collect no data in the field, no matter how good the software is.
  3. The warning and the event are at different resolutions. ICAR-NIVEDI's NADRES v2 forecasts disease risk at district × month granularity. Outbreaks actually propagate at village × day. That is a 30–100× resolution gap between the alarm and the thing it is meant to warn about.
  4. Vertical data silos with no shared keys. Dispensary registers, laboratory results, vaccination drives under LHDCP, milk-cooperative records, and human-health surveillance under IDSP each hold part of the picture and never join on a common animal, village or time key.
  5. Connectivity and literacy constraints are the design, not a footnote. Filling an English web form on 2G, standing in a cattle shed at six in the morning, is not a workflow anyone completes. Marathi voice input and genuine offline capability are the product here.
  6. Veterinarian density. Many blocks have a single veterinarian responsible for several thousand livestock units. The scarce resource being rationed is attention — which means triage, not diagnosis, is the function that actually needs automating.

What are the current challenges or inefficiencies?Where effort is being wasted right now

  • Duplicate manual entry. The same case is written into a paper register, a WhatsApp message to the block officer, and later into a government app — three times, by three people, with no reconciliation.
  • Blanket rather than targeted response. Because no one knows where the outbreak actually is, vaccine allocation defaults to uniform district-wise distribution instead of ring vaccination around confirmed clusters.
  • Laboratory referrals disappear. A sample is collected and sent; nobody — least of all the farmer — can see where it is or whether a result came back. The confirmation often arrives after the response window has closed.
  • No memory in the call channel. The 1962 animal-ambulance call centre handles calls one at a time; twenty calls from one village do not compose into an outbreak signal anywhere in the system.
  • Advisories are generic and late. Farmers receive broadcast advice, not "this is happening in your village, here is what to do in the next 48 hours" in their own language.
  • Vaccination and treatment histories are incomplete. Without a reliable per-animal history, you cannot tell an outbreak in an unvaccinated pocket from vaccine failure — two situations demanding opposite responses.
02

Feasibility of Execution

Can a working prototype be built in the hackathon timeline?Yes — and that is precisely the trap

The software here is easy. The credibility is hard. Every component we need is well-trodden: a progressive web app, a Postgres database, a map, a small classifier, a rules engine. A competent team ships the whole loop in 36–48 hours. What separates a winning build from the twenty other teams who also shipped a working app is domain accuracy — whether a veterinarian on the panel believes we understand the medicine and the reporting chain. Budget roughly two of five preparation days to domain research, not code.

What could be blockers?Name these before a judge names them for us

Four real blockers, with our stated position on each
  1. No public real-time outbreak feed exists. The data.gov.in livestock disease datasets are aggregate and largely cover 2005–2012. We cannot train on live outbreak data because live outbreak data is not published. Position: this gap is the problem statement, and we say so.
  2. Bharat Pashudhan has no open public API. Integration is a policy decision by DAHD, not something we can engineer around. Position: we key on the same 12-digit Tag ID and build an adapter layer, so integration becomes a configuration change rather than a rewrite.
  3. Regulatory constraint on diagnosis. An AI system must not present itself as diagnosing an animal; prescribing treatment is the legal preserve of a registered veterinary practitioner. Position: we triage and route, a vet confirms. This is designed in, not bolted on.
  4. The reporting disincentive. Farmers have economic reasons not to report. Position: this is solved socially — through compensation-claim value, anonymous syndrome reporting, and service-before-inspection — not technically.

Secondary risks worth tracking: image datasets for lesions are small and biased toward severe cases; Marathi ASR accuracy drops sharply on regional dialects such as Varhadi and Ahirani; and any live telephony demo depends on network conditions in the venue, so we rehearse a fallback.

Blockchain, IoT collars, a marketplace, an insurance module, Aadhaar login, and any claim to predict all fifteen notifiable diseases.

The demo that wins the room

Ninety seconds, in this order: (1) phone in airplane mode, farmer speaks a symptom in Marathi, report is filed locally. (2) Network restored, report syncs. (3) On the officer console, this report tips a village over the detection threshold and a cluster alarm fires with a visible p-value. (4) Officer taps the alert; an MVU is dispatched and a Marathi advisory goes out to the containment radius. (5) A sample QR is scanned into the lab pipeline, and the original farmer's screen updates with the status. One continuous story, no slide transitions.

Who benefits from this solution?Five beneficiary groups, with the specific benefit named

  • Smallholder farmers. For a household keeping two or three animals, a single preventable death removes a large share of annual income. Faster triage is direct income protection, and the case record doubles as evidence for a compensation claim.
  • Field veterinarians and para-veterinary workers. Triage converts an unsorted call list into a ranked queue. Mobile veterinary unit routing stops being first-come-first-served and starts being risk-weighted.
  • State and district government. Evidence-based vaccine allocation replaces uniform district-wise distribution; outbreak reporting becomes auditable and WOAH-compliant; and planning gets a real denominator instead of anecdote.
  • Public health system. Roughly six in ten emerging human infectious diseases are zoonotic in origin. For anthrax, brucellosis, avian influenza and leptospirosis, the animal signal precedes the human one — so an animal-side early warning is a human-side early warning.
  • Dairy cooperatives and insurers. Herd-level risk becomes measurable and therefore underwritable, which is a precondition for meaningful livestock insurance penetration.

What real-world impact can it create?Economic, social and systemic

Is it scalable beyond a hackathon?Unusually so, because the rails already exist

This is not a proposal that requires new infrastructure to be built before it can work. Bharat Pashudhan already supplies the animal identity layer, with roughly 36 crore animals tagged. LHDCP (formerly NADCP) already runs the national vaccination programme, having delivered around 147 crore FMD doses. The 1962 mobile veterinary unit call centre already supplies a dispatch fleet operating on a hybrid model of pre-set routes and on-demand calls. NADRES v2 already supplies a seasonal risk prior for fifteen diseases.

Our system is the connective tissue between them. That means state rollout is an integration project rather than a greenfield build — a materially cheaper and faster proposition than anything a competing team can offer. We should say this explicitly in the pitch, because it converts "nice prototype" into "deployable next financial year".

Why would evaluators find this problem important?Because it is their own recent failure

The strongest single fact about this problem statement

The sponsoring organisation is the Government of Maharashtra — a state that lost 34,711 cattle to Lumpy Skin Disease and faced a High Court petition seeking a proper policy response. This is not a hypothetical civic-technology exercise to the people judging it. It is a recent, expensive and politically visible failure that they were personally close to. Any solution that credibly shortens the detection-to-containment window speaks directly to that memory.

The gap, stated in one sentence

India has a system of record (Bharat Pashudhan) and a system of prediction (NADRES). It does not have a system of detection and response. Nothing today turns forty farmer-reported symptoms into a village-level statistical alarm, a dispatched mobile veterinary unit, a tracked laboratory sample, and a Marathi advisory to the two hundred households inside the containment radius. That is what we build.

What new approach can be added?Five innovations that are real methods, not buzzwords

  1. Prospective space-time scan statistics for syndromic clustering. Kulldorff's space-time permutation model is what public-health epidemiology genuinely uses for outbreak detection. It works on sparse counts, requires no population denominator, and outputs a p-value and a cluster radius instead of an unexplainable score. Almost no hackathon team will do this — most will train a random forest on three hundred synthetic rows and hope nobody asks how. SaTScan is the reference implementation to mirror.
  2. Voice-first Marathi capture into a syndrome ontology, not free text. The pipeline is speech recognition, then intent extraction, then mapping into a controlled vocabulary: fever, skin nodules, lameness, oral or hoof lesions, sudden death, abortion storm, nasal or ocular discharge. Structured syndromes are what make clustering mathematically possible; free text is what makes it impossible.
  3. Confidence-gated triage that is architecturally incapable of diagnosing. The output is syndrome + risk band + route, never a disease name presented as fact. Below a confidence floor the system does not guess — it escalates to a human faster. This is simultaneously the clinically correct design and the complete answer to the regulatory question.
  4. A connectivity degradation ladder: PWA, then offline queue, then SMS codec, then IVR. One case object, four transports. This is demonstrated by putting the phone into airplane mode during the pitch rather than by describing it on a slide.
  5. A One Health bridge into human surveillance. Zoonotic-suspect clusters emit a parallel notice on shared LGD geography, because the animal signal is the leading indicator. This is the state-of-the-art framing in global health and reads as genuinely sophisticated to a domain judge.

Where can teams misinterpret the PS?Five traps, in order of how many teams will fall into them

  1. Building a diagnosis app. The problem statement says triage, flag suspected outbreaks, and referral. It never asks anyone to diagnose. A team that promises "our AI detects twenty cattle diseases from a photograph" will be dismantled by the veterinary judge, and deservedly.
  2. Building a farmer super-app. Marketplace, insurance, breeding, feed advisory, weather, mandi prices. Scope creep reads to a judge as the absence of a thesis.
  3. Treating offline capability as a checkbox. The PS names low-connectivity operation explicitly, and judges from a state animal-husbandry department will ask for a disconnected demonstration. Offline is the differentiator, not the footnote.
  4. Ignoring the existing government systems. If we do not mention Bharat Pashudhan and NADRES unprompted, the panel concludes we did not research the domain — and everything else we say is discounted from that point on.
  5. Optimising the dashboard. A beautiful map that dispatches nobody is a screensaver. Every alert must carry an owner, an action and a time bound, or the dashboard is decoration.

Two subtler misreadings worth flagging: the phrase "at village, block and district levels" means the system must aggregate across three administrative tiers, not just plot points; and "coordinated response" means multiple agencies acting on one shared case object, which is a workflow requirement rather than a reporting one.

How should the solution be framed?One positioning sentence and one diagram

Open with this and never lose it

"Bharat Pashudhan records what happened. NADRES predicts what might happen next month. We detect what is happening this week, in this village — and dispatch the response."

Follow it immediately with the loop on a single slide: report → cluster → dispatch → sample → confirm → advisory → verified containment, drawn as one closed path. Alignment with the problem statement is proven by that loop closing, not by counting features. Then spend the remaining time in a live demonstration rather than on architecture diagrams — the architecture slide is for the appendix, to be produced only if asked.

What are the red flags evaluators might notice?Eight things that lose points instantly

Avoid every one of these
  • Accuracy claims such as 94% or 97% on a dataset scraped from Kaggle two days earlier, with no discussion of the train/test split.
  • A demo video instead of a live run. Panels read this as a confession that the build does not work.
  • "Blockchain for vaccination records" with no answer to why a signed database row would not do the same job.
  • An English-only interface in a problem statement sponsored by the Government of Maharashtra.
  • No mention of Bharat Pashudhan. Instant and probably unrecoverable loss of credibility with the government judge.
  • No answer to "who enters the data and why would they bother". This is the question that exposes whether we thought about the field or only about the screen.
  • A map with dots and no downstream action. Visualisation presented as if it were a decision.
  • Claiming zoonotic detection without being able to name a single zoonosis and its animal-side presentation.

One further warning: do not overstate what has been integrated. Saying "we integrated with Bharat Pashudhan" when there is no public API is a claim the government judge can falsify from memory, and being caught on it discredits every other claim in the pitch. The honest version — "we key on the same Tag ID and built an adapter, so integration is a configuration change when the API opens" — is both true and more impressive.

Role 5 is worth more than a second ML engineer, because this problem is decided on domain credibility and every judge on the panel can tell within sixty seconds whether we have it. If the team is smaller than six, merge roles 1 and 4, and keep role 5 intact.

Step-by-step approach before solution buildingResearch and ideation, in sequence

Days 1–2
Read the incumbents until we find the seam

Install the Bharat Pashudhan app and use it. Open NADRES v2 and read one monthly forewarning bulletin end to end. Read the PRS summary on Lumpy Skin Disease. We are looking for the seam between these systems, not for inspiration. Write down, in one paragraph each, exactly what each system does and does not do.

Days 2–3
Talk to two humans

One field veterinarian or veterinary-college faculty member, and one dairy farmer or para-veterinary worker. Ask exactly one question: "Walk me through the last time an animal in your village got sick — hour by hour." That transcript becomes both our demo script and our credibility on stage. Get permission to name them on a slide.

Day 3
Lock the disease scope to six

Chosen for Maharashtra relevance and visible clinical signs: LSD, FMD, Haemorrhagic Septicaemia, Black Quarter, PPR and anthrax — the last specifically for the zoonotic bridge. Write the rule-set as a YAML file and have the veterinary contact review it. Six diseases done credibly beats fifteen done vaguely.

Day 4
Design the case object once

A single schema covering report, animal, cluster, sample and advisory. Every channel — app, SMS, IVR, web — writes the same object. Get this right and the remaining build is mechanical; get it wrong and every channel becomes a separate codebase.

Day 5
Build the synthetic outbreak simulator first

Before any interface work. It is simultaneously our data source, our test harness and our demo engine, and it is the answer to the hardest data question a judge can ask. Details are in section 9.

Build phase
Build offline-first, then online

Write the local path first so that airplane mode is the default state rather than a special case retrofitted at the end. Retrofitting offline capability onto an online-first app in the last six hours does not work.

Final 6 hours
Freeze features and rehearse four times

No new features in the last six hours. Rehearse the full demonstration four times, including one deliberate failure recovery — kill the network mid-demo on purpose and continue — so that if it happens for real, it looks intentional.

AI-Buildability Split (20 / 80)

What can AI build fast, and what still needs us?The honest division of labour

AI builds this fast The 20%
  • CRUD API, database schema, authentication, migrations
  • React and PWA scaffolding, service-worker boilerplate
  • Leaflet map setup, choropleth rendering, marker clustering
  • Internationalisation plumbing and Marathi/Hindi string files
  • Chart components and dashboard layout
  • Docker, CI configuration, seed scripts, deployment
  • A first-pass image classifier over a public LSD/FMD dataset
  • The synthetic data generator scaffolding

This is roughly 70% of the keystrokes and 20% of the value. It produces a demo. It never produces a win.

Only judgment gets this right The 80%
  • The syndrome ontology — which clinical signs cluster to which suspicion, validated by a veterinarian
  • Detection thresholds — what count, in what radius, over what window, constitutes an outbreak rather than noise
  • Alert-fatigue economics — an officer who receives forty alerts ignores all forty
  • The reporting-disincentive problem — why a farmer files anything at all
  • Offline conflict semantics — two workers edit one animal offline; who wins, and can we defend the rule
  • Clinical safety gating — identifying where the model must refuse to answer
  • Government interoperability — LGD codes, Tag IDs, WOAH disease categories
  • The demo narrative — deciding which ninety seconds we actually show

What is the risk of leaning only on AI output?It fails exactly where this PS is judged

The specific failure mode to guard against

An AI-scaffolded build looks strongest precisely where this problem statement is judged weakest. It will happily generate a plausible-sounding symptom-to-disease mapping that a veterinarian recognises as wrong within ten seconds. It will produce a classifier whose reported accuracy comes from a leaky train/test split over near-duplicate images — a number that collapses the moment anyone asks how the split was made. And it will write confident advisory text that no clinician has reviewed, which is a genuine safety problem rather than a presentational one.

The domain judge's first question is always about the medicine, not the code. A team that cannot explain why fever plus skin nodules plus fly season points to Lumpy Skin Disease rather than pox has already lost the room, no matter how polished the application looks. Every team member must be able to defend the rule-set, not just the person who wrote it.

What structural change could a judge ask for on the spot?Three likely demands — and how to be able to say yes

Judges routinely test whether a system is genuinely built or merely demonstrated, by asking for a change live. For this problem statement, expect one of these three:

  • "Add Peste des Petits Ruminants for goats right now — show me."
  • "Change the alert aggregation unit from district to block, and re-run it."
  • "Turn the network off and file a report."

All three can be satisfied live, if we design for them on day one:

The demandThe design decision that makes it possibleWhat we do on stage
Add a new diseaseDisease rule-set lives in a YAML or JSON file hot-loaded at runtime, never compiled into application codePaste roughly twenty lines, refresh, and the new disease appears in triage
Change the aggregation unitAdministrative level is a parameter on the scan-statistic call, not a hardcoded columnChange one dropdown; the clusters recompute at block level
Work without a networkOffline path built first, so airplane mode is the default state rather than a special caseEnable airplane mode and complete a full report end to end
Why this is worth planning for deliberately

The moment one of these is satisfied live, the panel's assessment of everything else we claimed shifts — because we have just demonstrated that the system is real rather than staged. Conversely, a team that answers "we would need to change some code for that" confirms the opposite suspicion. Build these three affordances specifically because judges reach for them.

Is the data public, paid, or restricted — and how do we get it in time?

Everything we actually need is public and free. Nothing on the critical path is paid. The practical constraints are of three kinds. First, freshness: the open incidence data is a decade old, so it can inform a seasonal prior but cannot validate a detection system. Second, format: NADRES publishes PDFs rather than an API, so one team-member-day goes to writing a parser. Third, registration latency: Bhuvan and the data.gov.in API key require sign-up, so those should be requested on day one rather than the night before, with fallbacks identified in advance.

What happens if the ideal data source is not available?It is not available — and saying so is a strength

The honest answer to "where is your data?"

There is no public real-time livestock outbreak feed in India. Stating this confidently is a strength rather than an admission, because it is the problem statement. The data does not exist in accessible form precisely because the reporting system this PS asks us to build does not exist yet. Delivered as one clear sentence to the panel, it reframes an apparent gap as our central thesis — and it is far more persuasive than pretending we found a dataset we did not.

What is a realistic backup plan?A calibrated synthetic outbreak simulator, built on day one

Rather than generating arbitrary dummy rows, we build a simulator grounded in real inputs so that its output is defensible. Five ingredients:

  1. Real geography. Actual Maharashtra village LGD codes and centroids for one or two districts. Ahmednagar and Jalgaon are good choices — both were heavily affected during the 2022 LSD wave.
  2. Real denominators. Cattle population per district from the Livestock Census, so that incidence is expressed as a rate rather than a count.
  3. Real weather. Open-Meteo historical series for the outbreak window, feeding the same covariates our live model would use.
  4. A spatial SEIR transmission model with distance-decayed contact, using parameters drawn from published literature, calibrated so that its output reproduces the observed 2022 LSD spread pattern.
  5. Realistic noise. An under-reporting rate, a reporting-delay distribution, misclassified syndromes, and duplicate reports — because a detector that only works on clean data is not a detector.
The claim this earns us

"Our detection layer is validated against a simulator calibrated to the actual 2022 Maharashtra Lumpy Skin Disease wave. It flags the Ahmednagar cluster eleven days before the officially recorded first report."

That is a dramatically stronger claim than any accuracy percentage on a scraped image set — and critically, it is defensible, because we can show the calibration and let a judge inspect the parameters. It also converts our worst weakness (no real data) into a demonstrated capability (detection latency measured in days).

10

Judge Q&A Stress-Test

What is the weakest part a sharp judge targets first?

Our single most vulnerable point

"Who actually files the first report — and why would they?"

Every architecture in this space collapses on the cold-start problem. Our clustering is worthless without report volume; volume depends on farmer adoption; and adoption fights a real economic disincentive to report. This must be rehearsed by all six team members, not only the pitch lead, because it will be asked, and it will very likely be asked first. The full answer is Q3 below.

Q1Bharat Pashudhan already does animal-level records, vaccination and disease reporting, with 36 crore animals tagged. Why does your system exist?

Because Bharat Pashudhan is a system of record and we are a system of detection. It is a field-worker application — the farmer who sees the first symptom cannot enter anything into it, so the clock only starts when a para-vet next visits the village. It also stores reports as individual rows; nothing in it computes whether eleven reports from four adjacent villages over six days constitute a statistically significant cluster. We do not replace it. We key on the same 12-digit Tag ID and we are built to write back to it. If DAHD opens the API tomorrow, our integration is a configuration change, because we deliberately never invented our own animal identity.

Likely follow-up"And if the API never opens?" — State adoption first. Maharashtra's animal husbandry department runs its own dispensary and MVU network; we onboard through that and reconcile to Tag IDs by scanning the ear tag at the point of care.

Q2NADRES v2 is an award-winning AI forecasting system from ICAR-NIVEDI covering fifteen diseases. Isn't early warning already solved?

NADRES forecasts at district level, a month ahead, from historical incidence and meteorological covariates. It tells a Collector that a district is at elevated LSD risk in September, which is genuinely valuable — and we consume it as a Bayesian prior rather than competing with it. But an outbreak spreads village to village in days, and NADRES has no live field ingestion, so it cannot tell you the outbreak has actually started in Shevgaon on Tuesday. We are the likelihood term to their prior: they narrow the search space, our field reports localise the event. That composition is explicit in our design — our risk score is the prior multiplied by observed cluster significance.

Likely follow-up"How do you validate that the composition actually improves anything?" — Against our simulator we measure days-to-detection three ways: prior alone, field reports alone, and combined. The combination reduces latency because the prior lowers the alarm threshold exactly where background risk is already elevated, so fewer reports are needed to reach significance.

Q3Farmers have a strong incentive not to report — movement bans, market closure, culling. Your entire system depends on data they do not want to give.

We do not solve that with software alone, and we would be lying if we claimed otherwise. There are three levers. First, we make reporting pay: the report generates a case ID that becomes the farmer's documentary evidence for a state compensation claim. Maharashtra already compensates for LSD deaths, but the claim needs proof, and today that proof is a paper certificate obtained after the fact. Second, we allow syndrome-level anonymous reporting — a farmer can report clinical signs without immediately binding them to a tagged animal, which is sufficient for clustering, because clustering needs counts and locations, not identities. Third, the first thing the farmer receives back is service, an MVU dispatch or a veterinary callback, not an inspection. The behavioural design goal is that reporting feels like calling an ambulance, not calling the police.

Likely follow-up"Doesn't anonymous reporting let malicious or mistaken reports poison your clusters?" — Yes, which is why anonymous reports raise a signal but cannot alone trigger containment. A cluster escalates to action only after para-vet or MVU verification of at least one case, and we display that two-tier confidence in the alert itself so the officer knows what they are acting on.

Q4You are running AI on photographs of sick animals. What happens when it is wrong and a farmer loses a herd acting on your output?

Our system is architecturally incapable of telling a farmer a diagnosis, and that is a deliberate design choice rather than a disclaimer. The classifier emits a syndrome class and a confidence, and that feeds a routing decision — urgency band, who gets notified, whether a sample is requested. The farmer-facing message is always of the form: signs consistent with a notifiable condition, a veterinarian has been notified, do not move animals until examined. Below a confidence floor we do not guess — we escalate to a human faster. No treatment is ever recommended by the model, because prescribing treatment is the legal preserve of a registered veterinary practitioner. In effect the AI is deciding who looks at this and how soon, which is a triage decision, and triage systems are held to a very different and far more achievable standard than diagnostic devices.

Likely follow-up"What is your false-negative rate on that triage decision?" — We tune the threshold asymmetrically. A false positive costs a wasted veterinary visit; a false negative costs an outbreak. So we run deliberately high sensitivity, accept low precision, and report both numbers rather than a single accuracy figure.

Q5You claim it works offline. Show me. And what happens when two workers edit the same animal's record with no network?

[Put the phone into airplane mode and file a complete report with voice and photograph.] Every write is an append-only, locally-signed event carrying a device ID and a monotonic clock, queued in IndexedDB. The application never blocks on the network, because on a 2G shed floor the network is the exception rather than the rule. On reconnect we replay events rather than final states, so both workers' observations survive. For genuinely conflicting fields we resolve by role precedence — a veterinarian's entry supersedes a para-vet's, which supersedes a farmer's — and we surface the conflict in the record rather than silently discarding one side, because in a disease investigation the disagreement is itself clinical information. For zero-data areas we degrade further: the same case object encodes into a 140-character structured SMS, and an IVR keypad flow produces an identical record.

Likely follow-up"What is the maximum offline duration you have tested?" — A seven-day queue holding several hundred events including photographs, with photo compression and an LRU eviction policy on the media cache, so a prolonged outage degrades to text-only rather than failing outright.

Q6Six months after deployment, is this still running — or is it another pilot that quietly died?

The honest failure mode for systems like this is not technical. It is that data entry becomes unpaid extra work for a para-vet who already has a register to fill. So we designed the field worker's path to replace a task rather than add one: the report he files is simultaneously his visit log and his e-prescription record, which is what he currently writes twice. On funding, we are not asking for a new scheme — the LHDCP vaccination programme, the 1962 MVU call-centre contracts and the existing dispensary network are all already funded line items, and we are the coordination layer across them. And ownership sits with the district animal husbandry officer, whose own performance indicators — vaccination coverage, mortality, response time — are exactly what our dashboard reports. A system that makes its owner's numbers look better is the kind that survives.

Likely follow-up"What is your first pilot district, and why that one?" — Ahmednagar or Jalgaon: high cattle density, documented severe LSD impact in 2022, existing MVU coverage, and a veterinary college nearby for clinical validation of the rule-set.
Final Verdict

GREEN LIGHT

Take this problem statement — with one condition. Position ourselves as the detection and response layer that sits above Bharat Pashudhan and beside NADRES, never as a replacement for either. Build the offline path first, keep the disease rule-set as data rather than code, and construct the demonstration around a simulated replay of the 2022 Maharashtra Lumpy Skin Disease wave.

The single biggest reason for this verdict

The winning artifact here is systems design and last-mile user experience, not an AI breakthrough — which means a disciplined team can genuinely finish it inside a hackathon rather than merely gesturing at it. And the sponsoring government has a recent, expensive, politically visible failure — 34,711 cattle lost in Maharashtra alone — that this addresses directly. The one thing that could sink us, being dismissed as "Bharat Pashudhan with a nicer map", is entirely avoidable, and avoiding it costs nothing but the first sentence of the pitch.
